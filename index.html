01Matter, Measurement & Energy

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. It exists in three common states: solid (definite shape and volume), liquid (definite volume, takes shape of container), and gas (fills entire container, no definite shape or volume). All changes in matter involve energy, which is the ability to do work or produce heat.

Classification of Matter

CategoryDefinitionExamples
ElementPure substance made of one type of atom; cannot be broken down furtherO, Fe, Au, C
CompoundPure substance made of two or more elements in a fixed ratio, chemically bondedH₂O, NaCl, CO₂
Homogeneous mixtureUniform composition throughout (solution)Saltwater, air, brass
Heterogeneous mixtureNon-uniform; distinct regions visibleSalad, sand + water, granite

Physical vs. Chemical Properties & Changes

TypeDefinitionExamples
Physical propertyObserved without changing the substance's identityColor, boiling point, density, mass
Chemical propertyDescribes how a substance reacts or changes into a new substanceFlammability, reactivity with acid, rusting
Physical changeAppearance changes but identity stays the sameMelting ice, dissolving sugar, cutting paper
Chemical changeNew substance(s) formed; usually irreversibleBurning, rusting, cooking an egg
Signs of a chemical change: color change, gas production (bubbles), precipitate formation, temperature change, light or odor produced.

Energy Basics

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion. Potential energy is stored energy (chemical bonds, position, etc.). Energy is always conserved (law of conservation of energy): it can change forms but cannot be created or destroyed. Exothermic reactions release heat to the surroundings. Endothermic reactions absorb heat from the surroundings.

Temperature Conversions
°C = (°F - 32) × 5/9
K = °C + 273

Water freezes at 0°C / 32°F / 273 K. Water boils at 100°C / 212°F / 373 K. Absolute zero = 0 K.
Density
D = m / V
Units: g/mL or g/cm³. Water density = 1.00 g/mL. Objects denser than water sink.

02Atomic Structure

All matter is composed of atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element. Atoms are made of three subatomic particles.

ParticleChargeLocationMass (amu)
Proton (p+)+1Nucleus~1
Neutron (n)0Nucleus~1
Electron (e-)-1Electron cloud (orbitals)~0 (1/1836 amu)
Key Relationships
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons = identifies the element
Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons
Neutrons = mass number - atomic number
In a neutral atom: protons = electrons

Isotopes & Atomic Mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons (same Z, different A). For example, carbon-12 has 6 neutrons and carbon-14 has 8 neutrons. The atomic mass on the periodic table is a weighted average of all naturally occurring isotopes.

Electron Configuration (Simplified)

Electrons fill energy levels (shells) around the nucleus. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second holds up to 8, the third holds up to 18 (but fills to 8 first in most introductory contexts). Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost shell and are responsible for bonding and chemical behavior.

Remember
The number of valence electrons for main-group elements equals the group number (using the 1A-8A system). Group 1A has 1 valence electron, Group 7A has 7, Group 8A (noble gases) has 8 (full shell).

Ions

An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons. Metals tend to lose electrons and form cations (positive charge). Nonmetals tend to gain electrons and form anions (negative charge). Ions form to achieve a stable, noble-gas electron configuration (octet).

The periodic table arranges elements by increasing atomic number. Rows are called periods and columns are called groups (families). Elements in the same group share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons.

04Chemical Bonding

Atoms bond to achieve a stable, full outer electron shell (octet rule: 8 electrons for most, 2 for hydrogen). The type of bond depends on whether electrons are transferred or shared.

Bond TypeBetweenWhat HappensExample
IonicMetal + nonmetalElectrons transferred; ions attractNaCl (Na+ and Cl-)
CovalentNonmetal + nonmetalElectrons shared between atomsH₂O, CO₂, CH₄
MetallicMetal + metalElectrons delocalized in a "sea"Cu wire, Fe nail

Ionic vs. Covalent Properties

PropertyIonic CompoundsCovalent (Molecular) Compounds
State at room tempSolid (crystalline)Gas, liquid, or soft solid
Melting/boiling pointHighLow
Conductivity (dissolved/melted)Yes (free ions)No (no ions)
Solubility in waterOften solubleVaries

Polar vs. Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

When two atoms share electrons equally, the bond is nonpolar covalent (same atom or very similar electronegativity, like H₂ or O₂). When electrons are shared unequally (one atom is more electronegative), the bond is polar covalent (like H-O in water). The greater the electronegativity difference, the more polar the bond. Very large differences (>1.7) typically indicate ionic bonding.

Lewis Dot Structures

Lewis dot structures show valence electrons as dots around an element symbol. In a molecule, shared pairs form bonds (lines) and unshared pairs are lone pairs. The total number of valence electrons in the structure must equal the sum from all atoms. Hydrogen always forms 1 bond, oxygen typically forms 2, nitrogen 3, and carbon 4.

05Nomenclature

Naming chemical compounds follows different rules depending on whether the compound is ionic or covalent.

Ionic Compounds (Metal + Nonmetal)

Name the metal first (cation), then the nonmetal with an "-ide" ending (anion). If the metal can have multiple charges (transition metals), use a Roman numeral to indicate the charge. Examples: NaCl = sodium chloride, FeCl₂ = iron(II) chloride, FeCl₃ = iron(III) chloride.

Covalent Compounds (Nonmetal + Nonmetal)

Use Greek prefixes to indicate the number of each atom. The first element does not get a prefix if there is only one of it. The second element always gets an "-ide" ending. Examples: CO₂ = carbon dioxide, N₂O₄ = dinitrogen tetroxide, PCl₃ = phosphorus trichloride.

Acids

Binary acids (H + one nonmetal): "hydro-" + root + "-ic acid" (HCl = hydrochloric acid). Oxyacids (H + polyatomic ion with oxygen): if the ion ends in "-ate," the acid ends in "-ic acid" (H₂SO₄ = sulfuric acid). If the ion ends in "-ite," the acid ends in "-ous acid" (H₂SO₃ = sulfurous acid).

06Chemical Reactions & Equations

A chemical equation represents a chemical reaction using formulas. Reactants are on the left of the arrow, products are on the right. Equations must be balanced so that the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides (law of conservation of mass).

Balancing tip: Adjust coefficients (the numbers in front of formulas), never subscripts. Balance metals first, then nonmetals, then hydrogen, then oxygen last.

Five Major Reaction Types

TypeGeneral FormExample
Synthesis (combination)A + B → AB2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl
DecompositionAB → A + B2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
Single replacementA + BC → AC + BZn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
Double replacementAB + CD → AD + CBAgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃
CombustionFuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂OCH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
Activity Series Shortcut
In single replacement, the free element must be more reactive than the element it replaces. Metals higher on the activity series replace metals lower on the series. If a metal is below hydrogen in the activity series, it cannot replace hydrogen from acids.

Exothermic vs. Endothermic

Exothermic reactions release energy (feel hot, products are more stable). Endothermic reactions absorb energy (feel cold, products are less stable). Combustion is always exothermic. Photosynthesis is endothermic. Melting ice is endothermic.

07The Mole & Stoichiometry

The mole is the chemist's counting unit. One mole of anything contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number). The molar mass of an element (in g/mol) is numerically equal to its atomic mass on the periodic table. The molar mass of a compound is the sum of the molar masses of all atoms in the formula.

Core Mole Conversions
moles = grams / molar mass
grams = moles × molar mass
particles = moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry uses a balanced equation to convert between moles (or grams) of one substance and moles (or grams) of another. The coefficients in the balanced equation give the mole ratios. The general approach is: grams of A → moles of A → mole ratio → moles of B → grams of B.

Limiting reagent: the reactant that runs out first and determines how much product can be made. The other reactant is in excess. To find the limiting reagent, convert each reactant to moles of product and see which gives less.
Percent Yield
% yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100
Theoretical yield is calculated from stoichiometry. Actual yield is measured in the lab. Percent yield is always ≤ 100%.

08Gases

Gases have no definite shape or volume and are easily compressed. Gas behavior is described by several laws relating pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), and amount (n).

LawRelationshipConstantEquation
Boyle'sP and V are inversely proportionalT, nP₁V₁ = P₂V₂
Charles'sV and T are directly proportionalP, nV₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
Gay-Lussac'sP and T are directly proportionalV, nP₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂
Avogadro'sV and n are directly proportionalP, TV₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂
Ideal Gas Law
PV = nRT

R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K. Temperature must always be in Kelvin (K = °C + 273). At STP (0°C, 1 atm), 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 L.
Remember
Boyle's: pressure goes up, volume goes down (squeeze a balloon). Charles's: heat it, it expands (hot air balloon). Gas law problems always use Kelvin for temperature.

09Solutions & Concentration

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of a solute (the substance dissolved) and a solvent (the substance doing the dissolving, usually water). Concentration describes how much solute is dissolved in a given amount of solution.

Molarity
M = moles of solute / liters of solution
Units: mol/L (M). The most common way to express concentration in chemistry.
Dilution
M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
Use this when diluting a concentrated solution with solvent. The moles of solute stay the same.

Solubility Factors

"Like dissolves like" is the key rule. Polar solutes dissolve in polar solvents (sugar in water). Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents (oil in hexane). Increasing temperature generally increases solubility of solids in liquids but decreases solubility of gases. Increasing pressure increases gas solubility (Henry's law).

Electrolytes

A strong electrolyte dissociates completely in water and conducts electricity well (NaCl, HCl, NaOH). A weak electrolyte only partially dissociates (acetic acid, ammonia). A nonelectrolyte does not dissociate at all (sugar, ethanol).

pH Scale
pH = -log[H⁺]

pH 7 = neutral. pH < 7 = acidic. pH > 7 = basic. Each pH unit represents a 10-fold change in [H⁺]. pH 3 is 10 times more acidic than pH 4 and 100 times more acidic than pH 5.

Strong vs. Weak

Strong Acids (memorize)Strong Bases (memorize)
HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂

Strong acids and bases dissociate completely in water. Weak acids (like acetic acid, CH₃COOH) and weak bases (like ammonia, NH₃) only partially dissociate.

Neutralization

Neutralization Reaction
acid + base → salt + water
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

A buffer is a solution that resists pH change when small amounts of acid or base are added. Buffers contain a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid).

11Nuclear Chemistry

Nuclear chemistry involves changes in the nucleus of an atom. Unlike chemical reactions (which involve electrons), nuclear reactions change the number of protons or neutrons, transmuting one element into another.

Types of Radioactive Decay

TypeParticleSymbolPenetrating PowerEffect on Nucleus
Alpha (α)Helium nucleus⁴₂HeLow (paper stops it)Mass # drops 4, atomic # drops 2
Beta (β)Electron⁰-₁eMedium (aluminum stops it)Neutron → proton; atomic # rises 1
Gamma (γ)High-energy photonγHigh (lead/concrete)No change in mass or atomic number
Half-Life
Amount remaining = initial amount × (1/2)^n
n = number of half-lives = total time / half-life

After 1 half-life, 50% remains. After 2, 25%. After 3, 12.5%. After 4, 6.25%.

Nuclear Reactions

TypeDescriptionExample
FissionLarge nucleus splits into smaller nuclei; releases energyNuclear power plants, atomic bombs (U-235)
FusionSmall nuclei combine into a larger nucleus; releases even more energySun, hydrogen bombs (H → He)
Both fission and fusion release energy because the products have less mass than the reactants. The missing mass is converted to energy via Einstein's E = mc². Fusion releases more energy per gram but requires extreme temperatures.
