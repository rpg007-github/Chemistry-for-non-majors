01 Matter & Energy — What Everything Is Made Of

Definition: Matter

Matter is anything that has mass (weight) and takes up space. Your phone, a glass of water, the air you breathe — all matter. The only things that are NOT matter are pure energy forms like light or heat.

Matter can exist in three common states:

StateShapeVolumeEveryday Example
SolidFixed (keeps its shape)Fixed (same size)Ice cube, rock, wooden table
LiquidTakes shape of containerFixed (same amount)Water in a glass, milk, juice
GasFills entire containerExpands to fill spaceSteam, air in a tire, helium in a balloon

Classifying Matter: What type of stuff is it?

Definition: Element

A pure substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. There are 118 known elements (hydrogen, gold, oxygen, carbon, etc.).

Definition: Compound

A pure substance made of two or more elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. Water is always 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom — never more, never less.

Definition: Mixture

Two or more substances physically combined (not chemically bonded). You can usually separate them without a chemical reaction. Mixtures can vary in their composition.

Real-World Examples

Element: Pure gold jewelry (only gold atoms), oxygen gas you breathe (only oxygen atoms)

Compound: Table salt (sodium + chlorine, always 1:1), water (hydrogen + oxygen, always 2:1)

Homogeneous mixture (uniform throughout): Saltwater, air, black coffee — looks the same everywhere

Heterogeneous mixture (not uniform): Italian salad dressing (oil + water layers you can see), trail mix, a bowl of cereal

Physical vs. Chemical Changes

Definition: Physical Change

The substance looks different but is still the same substance. No new substance is created. It is usually reversible.

Definition: Chemical Change

A new substance with new properties is created. Usually cannot be easily reversed. Also called a chemical reaction.

Physical vs. Chemical — Easy Examples

Physical: Melting ice (still water), cutting paper (still paper), dissolving sugar in tea (still sugar + water)

Chemical: Burning wood (turns to ash + smoke — NOT wood anymore), rusting iron, baking a cake, digesting food

How to Spot a Chemical Change

Look for: a color change that wasn't expected, bubbles of gas forming, a solid suddenly appearing in a liquid (called a precipitate), a big temperature change, light being produced, or a new smell. If you see these, a chemical reaction probably happened.

Energy Basics

Definition: Energy

The ability to do work or cause change. Energy is never created or destroyed — it just changes form. This is called the Law of Conservation of Energy.

TermMeaningExample
Kinetic energyEnergy of motion — anything that is moving has itA rolling bowling ball, a molecule bouncing around
Potential energyStored energy — waiting to be usedA book on a shelf, energy stored in a chemical bond
Exothermic reactionReleases heat to surroundings — feels hotBurning wood, hand warmers, combustion in a car engine
Endothermic reactionAbsorbs heat from surroundings — feels coldMelting ice, instant cold packs, cooking (absorbs heat from stove)
Temperature Conversion Formulas
°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 K = °C + 273
Water freezes: 0°C / 32°F / 273 K  |  Water boils: 100°C / 212°F / 373 K  |  Absolute zero: 0 K (coldest possible)
Density Formula
Density = Mass ÷ Volume     (D = m/V)
Units: grams per mL (g/mL). Water's density = 1.00 g/mL. If something is denser than 1.00 g/mL, it sinks. If less, it floats. That's why wood floats and rocks sink!
✎ Worked Example — Density — Does It Float or Sink?
A block of wood has a mass of 45 g and a volume of 60 mL. A rock has a mass of 120 g and a volume of 40 mL. Calculate the density of each, and predict which floats and which sinks in water (water's density = 1.00 g/mL).
1Write the formula: Density = mass ÷ volume (D = m/V)
2Wood: D = 45 g ÷ 60 mL = 0.75 g/mL. This is less than 1.00, so the wood floats.
3Rock: D = 120 g ÷ 40 mL = 3.0 g/mL. This is greater than 1.00, so the rock sinks.
4The rule: Density < 1.00 g/mL → floats. Density > 1.00 g/mL → sinks. Density = 1.00 g/mL → hovers in the middle.
✓ Answer: Wood: 0.75 g/mL (floats). Rock: 3.0 g/mL (sinks).
✎ Worked Example — Temperature Conversion — Three Ways
A patient has a fever of 103°F. A chemistry lab calls for a reaction at 25°C. Convert 103°F to Celsius and Kelvin. Convert 25°C to Fahrenheit and Kelvin.
1103°F → Celsius: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 = (103 − 32) × 5/9 = 71 × 5/9 = 355/9 ≈ 39.4°C. (Normal body temp is 37°C — 39.4°C is indeed a fever!)
239.4°C → Kelvin: K = °C + 273 = 39.4 + 273 = 312.4 K
325°C → Fahrenheit: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32 = (25 × 9/5) + 32 = 45 + 32 = 77°F. (Room temperature — comfortable!)
425°C → Kelvin: K = 25 + 273 = 298 K
5Memory trick: 0°C = 32°F = 273 K (freezing). 100°C = 212°F = 373 K (boiling). These anchors help you check if your answer is reasonable.
✓ Answer: 103°F = 39.4°C = 312.4 K.   25°C = 77°F = 298 K.

02 Atoms — The Building Blocks of Everything

Definition: Atom

The smallest unit of an element that still has the chemical properties of that element. Everything you can touch is made of atoms. Atoms are incredibly tiny — a single human hair is about 500,000 atoms wide.

Every atom has a nucleus (a dense center) surrounded by a cloud of electrons. The nucleus contains protons and neutrons.

ParticleChargeLocationMassThink of it as...
Proton+1 (positive)Inside nucleus~1 amuThe particle that defines WHAT element it is
Neutron0 (neutral)Inside nucleus~1 amuAdds mass, helps hold nucleus together
Electron−1 (negative)Orbiting outsideEssentially 0Responsible for all chemical bonding and reactions
The Most Important Numbers

Atomic number = number of protons. This is what makes an element unique. Carbon always has 6 protons. Oxygen always has 8 protons. If you change the protons, you change the element.

Mass number = protons + neutrons. This tells you how heavy the atom is.

In a neutral atom: protons = electrons. If an atom gains or loses electrons, it becomes an ion (charged).

Example: Sodium (Na)

Atomic number = 11 (always has 11 protons). If the mass number is 23, then: neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

As a neutral atom, it has 11 electrons. If it loses 1 electron, it becomes Na⁺ (sodium ion, +1 charge).

Isotopes — Same Element, Different Weight

Definition: Isotope

Atoms of the same element (same number of protons) but with a different number of neutrons. They behave almost identically in chemical reactions but have different masses.

Carbon Isotopes Example

Carbon-12: 6 protons, 6 neutrons — the most common form of carbon

Carbon-14: 6 protons, 8 neutrons — radioactive; used in carbon dating to figure out how old ancient objects are

Valence Electrons — The Bonding Electrons

Definition: Valence Electrons

The electrons in the outermost shell of an atom. These are the only electrons involved in chemical bonding. Atoms "want" to have a full outer shell (usually 8 electrons — called the octet rule).

Quick Rule for Valence Electrons

For the main-group elements, the group number (using the old 1A–8A system) tells you the number of valence electrons. Group 1A = 1 valence electron. Group 6A = 6. Group 8A (noble gases) = 8 (full = stable = doesn't react).

Ions — Atoms with a Charge

Definition: Ion

An atom that has gained or lost electrons, giving it an electrical charge. Metals tend to lose electrons (become positive). Nonmetals tend to gain electrons (become negative).

TermMeaningExample
CationPositive ion (lost electrons)Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ — metals always form cations
AnionNegative ion (gained electrons)Cl⁻, O²⁻, F⁻ — nonmetals form anions
✎ Worked Example — Finding Protons, Neutrons, Electrons — and Ion Charges
Use the periodic table to answer: (A) A neutral calcium atom has atomic number 20 and mass number 40. Find its protons, neutrons, and electrons. (B) Calcium loses 2 electrons to form an ion. Write the ion formula and give its charge. (C) Chlorine (atomic number 17, mass number 35) gains 1 electron. Write its ion.
1Part A — neutral calcium: Protons = atomic number = 20. Neutrons = mass number − protons = 40 − 20 = 20. Electrons = protons (neutral atom) = 20.
2Part B — calcium ion: Loses 2 electrons: 20 electrons − 2 = 18 electrons. Charge = protons − electrons = 20 − 18 = +2. Ion formula: Ca²⁺. (Metals lose electrons → positive ions = cations.)
3Part C — chloride ion: Gains 1 electron: 17 electrons + 1 = 18 electrons. Charge = 17 − 18 = −1. Ion formula: Cl⁻. (Nonmetals gain electrons → negative ions = anions.)
4Notice: Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ now both have 18 electrons — the same as the noble gas argon (Ar, #18). Atoms gain/lose electrons to reach this stable, full-shell configuration!
✓ Answer: Ca: 20p, 20n, 20e (neutral); Ca²⁺ (lost 2e, charge +2). Cl⁻ (gained 1e, charge −1).

03 The Periodic Table — The Chemist's Map

Definition: Periodic Table

A chart that organizes all known elements by increasing atomic number. Elements in the same column (group/family) have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of valence electrons. Elements in the same row (period) have the same number of electron shells.

The Three Main Regions

RegionWhere on the TableKey PropertiesExamples
MetalsLeft side and centerShiny, conduct electricity and heat, can be hammered into shapes (malleable), tend to lose electronsIron (Fe), copper (Cu), gold (Au), sodium (Na)
NonmetalsUpper right cornerDull, poor conductors, tend to gain or share electronsOxygen (O), carbon (C), nitrogen (N), chlorine (Cl)
MetalloidsStaircase line between metals and nonmetalsProperties of both — used in semiconductors (computer chips!)Silicon (Si), germanium (Ge)

Important Families to Know

GroupFamily NameValence e⁻What you need to know
1AAlkali metals1Very reactive! They react violently with water. Form +1 ions. (Li, Na, K)
2AAlkaline earth metals2Reactive, form +2 ions. (Mg, Ca)
7AHalogens7Very reactive nonmetals — they desperately want 1 more electron. Form −1 ions. (F, Cl, Br, I)
8ANoble gases8 (full!)Already have a full outer shell — almost never react with anything. (He, Ne, Ar)

Periodic Trends — Patterns Across the Table

Properties change in predictable ways as you move across a period (left→right) or down a group (top→bottom).

PropertyAcross a period →Down a group ↓Why it happens
Atomic radius (atom size)Gets smallerGets largerMore protons pull electrons in (→). More electron shells added (↓).
Ionization energy (energy to remove an electron)IncreasesDecreasesSmaller atoms hold electrons more tightly.
Electronegativity (ability to attract electrons in a bond)IncreasesDecreasesFluorine (top right) is the most electronegative element — it pulls electrons hardest.
Diatomic Elements — These Always Come in Pairs

Seven elements are never found alone in nature — they always form two-atom molecules. Remember: HOFBrINCl (say "Hoff-brinkle"). That's H₂, O₂, F₂, Br₂, I₂, N₂, Cl₂. When you write these in an equation, always use the subscript 2.

04 Chemical Bonding — How Atoms Stick Together

Why Do Atoms Bond?

Atoms bond with each other to become more stable — specifically, to get a full outer shell of 8 electrons (the octet rule). Hydrogen is special: it only needs 2 electrons.

The Two Main Types of Bonds

Bond TypeWho forms it?What happens to electrons?Common examples
Ionic BondMetal + nonmetalElectrons are transferred from metal to nonmetal, creating opposite charges that attractNaCl (table salt), MgO, CaCl₂
Covalent BondNonmetal + nonmetalElectrons are shared between atomsH₂O (water), CO₂, CH₄ (natural gas)
Metallic BondMetal + metalElectrons float freely through the whole material ("sea of electrons")Copper wire, iron, gold
Ionic Bond Example: Table Salt (NaCl)

Sodium (Na) has 1 valence electron it "wants" to give away. Chlorine (Cl) has 7 valence electrons and "wants" 1 more to fill its shell. So Na gives its electron to Cl. Na becomes Na⁺ (positive), Cl becomes Cl⁻ (negative). Opposites attract — they stick together!

Covalent Bond Example: Water (H₂O)

Oxygen needs 2 more electrons to fill its shell. Hydrogen each need 1 more. So 2 hydrogen atoms each share 1 electron with the oxygen atom. Nobody fully gives or takes — they share. Result: a water molecule.

Properties: Ionic vs. Covalent Compounds

PropertyIonic CompoundsCovalent Compounds
State at room temperatureSolid (crystalline)Can be gas, liquid, or solid
Melting/boiling pointHigh (need lots of energy to break apart all those ions)Low (easier to separate)
Conducts electricity when dissolved?Yes — ions can carry chargeNo — no ions present
ExampleSalt, chalk, baking sodaSugar, water, carbon dioxide

Polar vs. Nonpolar Bonds

Polar vs. Nonpolar Covalent Bond

When two atoms share electrons equally (same or very similar electronegativity), the bond is nonpolar. When electrons are shared unequally (one atom pulls harder), the bond is polar — one end is slightly negative (δ⁻) and the other is slightly positive (δ⁺).

Polar Bond Example: Water

Oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen, so it pulls the shared electrons closer to itself. This makes the oxygen end of the water molecule slightly negative and the hydrogen ends slightly positive. This is why water dissolves so many things — it's like a tiny magnet!

Quick Rule: What Kind of Bond?

Metal + nonmetal → ionic bond (electrons transferred)
Nonmetal + nonmetal → covalent bond (electrons shared)
Very large electronegativity difference (>1.7) → ionic character

Molecular Shape (VSEPR — Quick Version)

Lone pairs (unshared electron pairs) take up space and push bonded atoms around, changing the shape of a molecule.

Bonded atomsLone pairsShapeExampleThink of it as...

✎ Worked Example — Writing Ionic Formulas — The Crisscross Method
Write the correct formulas for: (A) magnesium chloride, (B) aluminum oxide, (C) iron(III) sulfide. Struggling students: the secret is that the TOTAL positive charge must equal the TOTAL negative charge.
1The rule: Charges must balance to zero. The ion charges 'crisscross' to become the subscripts.
2A — Magnesium chloride: Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻. Charges: +2 and −1. To balance: need 2 chloride ions to cancel 1 magnesium. Formula: MgCl₂. Check: 1×(+2) + 2×(−1) = 0 ✓
3B — Aluminum oxide: Al³⁺ and O²⁻. Charges: +3 and −2. Crisscross: the 3 from Al becomes the subscript on O, the 2 from O becomes the subscript on Al → Al₂O₃. Check: 2×(+3) + 3×(−2) = +6 − 6 = 0 ✓. Formula: Al₂O₃
4C — Iron(III) sulfide: Fe³⁺ (the III tells you +3) and S²⁻ (sulfur gains 2). Same as aluminum oxide: crisscross → Fe₂S₃. Check: 2×(+3) + 3×(−2) = 0 ✓. Formula: Fe₂S₃
5Always simplify: If crisscross gives Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ → Ca₂O₂, simplify to CaO (divide both by 2). The charges already balance with 1:1 ratio.
✓ Answer: MgCl₂, Al₂O₃, Fe₂S₃. Always verify: total positive charge = total negative charge.

05 Naming Compounds — The Language of Chemistry

Different types of compounds follow different naming rules. First figure out: is this ionic (metal + nonmetal) or covalent (nonmetal + nonmetal)?

Ionic Compounds (Metal + Nonmetal)

Naming Rule: Ionic Compounds

1. Name the metal first (no change needed). 2. Name the nonmetal but change its ending to -ide. 3. If the metal is a transition metal that can have different charges, add a Roman numeral in parentheses to show the charge.

Ionic Naming Examples

NaCl → sodium chloride (sodium + chlorine → chloride)

MgO → magnesium oxide

FeCl₂ → iron(II) chloride — the (II) tells you iron has a +2 charge here

FeCl₃ → iron(III) chloride — the (III) tells you iron has a +3 charge here

Covalent Compounds (Nonmetal + Nonmetal)

Naming Rule: Covalent Compounds

Use Greek number prefixes to show how many of each atom there are. Change the ending of the second element to -ide. Don't use "mono-" for the first element if there is only one of it.

PrefixNumberMemory trick
mono-1monorail = 1 rail
di-2bicycle = 2 wheels
tri-3triangle = 3 sides
tetra-4tetrapod = 4 feet
penta-5pentagon = 5 sides
hexa-6hexagon = 6 sides
Covalent Naming Examples

CO₂ → carbon dioxide (2 oxygens)

N₂O₄ → dinitrogen tetroxide

PCl₃ → phosphorus trichloride

Polyatomic Ions — Groups of Atoms with a Charge

Definition: Polyatomic Ion

A group of atoms that are bonded together and carry an overall charge. They stay together as a unit in chemical reactions. You need to memorize the most common ones.

Ion FormulaNameCharge

✎ Worked Example — Naming Compounds — Formula to Name AND Name to Formula
Practice both directions. (A) Name these compounds: CaBr₂, N₂O₅, FeSO₄. (B) Write formulas for: sodium phosphate, dinitrogen monoxide, copper(II) nitrate.
1A — CaBr₂: Ca is a metal (Group 2A), Br is a nonmetal → ionic compound. Name metal: calcium. Name nonmetal with -ide: bromide. No Roman numeral needed (Ca is always +2). Name: calcium bromide.
2A — N₂O₅: Both nonmetals (N and O) → covalent compound, use prefixes. N₂ = dinitrogen. O₅ = pentoxide. Name: dinitrogen pentoxide.
3A — FeSO₄: Fe is a transition metal + SO₄²⁻ (sulfate, a polyatomic ion). SO₄²⁻ has charge −2, so Fe must be +2 to balance. Iron(II) + sulfate. Name: iron(II) sulfate.
4B — Sodium phosphate: Sodium = Na⁺ (+1). Phosphate = PO₄³⁻ (−3). Crisscross: 3 Na⁺ needed to balance 1 PO₄³⁻. Formula: Na₃PO₄. Check: 3(+1) + (−3) = 0 ✓
5B — Dinitrogen monoxide: Di = 2, mono = 1. Two nitrogen atoms, one oxygen. Formula: N₂O. (This is laughing gas!)
6B — Copper(II) nitrate: Cu²⁺ and NO₃⁻ (−1). Need 2 nitrate ions to balance Cu²⁺. Formula: Cu(NO₃)₂. Parentheses around the polyatomic ion when subscript > 1.
✓ Answer: CaBr₂ = calcium bromide. N₂O₅ = dinitrogen pentoxide. FeSO₄ = iron(II) sulfate. Na₃PO₄, N₂O, Cu(NO₃)₂.

06 Chemical Reactions & Equations

Definition: Chemical Equation

A shorthand way to write a chemical reaction using formulas. Reactants (starting materials) go on the left side. Products (what you end up with) go on the right side. The arrow means "reacts to form."

Reading an Equation

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O means: "2 molecules of hydrogen gas react with 1 molecule of oxygen gas to produce 2 molecules of water."

Balancing Equations — The Golden Rule

The number of each type of atom must be the same on both sides of the equation (Law of Conservation of Mass — matter cannot be created or destroyed). Adjust only the coefficients (the big numbers in front of formulas) — never change the subscripts inside a formula, because that would change what compound you have.

The Five Main Types of Reactions

TypePatternExampleThink of it as...
SynthesisA + B → AB2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaClTwo things combine into one
DecompositionAB → A + B2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂One thing breaks apart into two (or more)
Single ReplacementA + BC → AC + BZn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + CuOne element kicks another out and takes its place
Double ReplacementAB + CD → AD + CBAgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃Two compounds swap partners
CombustionFuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂OCH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂OSomething burns — always produces CO₂ and water, always releases heat
Reaction Types in Real Life

Synthesis: Your body building proteins from amino acids

Decomposition: Food rotting, hydrogen peroxide breaking down in a wound (the fizzing you see!)

Single replacement: Zinc metal dissolving in an acid, pushing out hydrogen gas

Double replacement: Mixing bleach and ammonia — dangerous! The ions swap and form new (toxic) compounds

Combustion: Burning gasoline in a car, candles, natural gas on a stove

✎ Worked Example — Balancing Equations Step by Step
Balance this equation:   __ Al + __ O₂ → __ Al₂O₃   (aluminum burning in oxygen). This is the most common type of balancing problem on exams.
1Step 1 — Count atoms on each side (unbalanced): Left: 1 Al, 2 O.   Right: 2 Al, 3 O.   Not balanced.
2Step 2 — Balance Al first (appears in fewer formulas): Right side has 2 Al. Put a 2 in front of Al on the left: 2 Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃. Now: Left: 2 Al, 2 O. Right: 2 Al, 3 O.
3Step 3 — Balance O: Right has 3 O. Left has 2 O (from O₂). We need 3 O on the left, but O₂ comes in pairs — we can't have 1.5 O₂. Multiply everything by 2: 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃.
4Step 4 — Check: Left: 4 Al, 6 O. Right: 2×2 = 4 Al, 2×3 = 6 O. Balanced! ✓
5Golden rule reminder: ONLY change the big coefficients (numbers in front). NEVER change the small subscripts inside a formula — that would change the compound itself!
✓ Answer: 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃

07 The Mole & Stoichiometry — Counting Atoms

Definition: The Mole

A unit chemists use to count atoms and molecules. Because atoms are so incredibly tiny, we need a very large counting unit. One mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (about 602 billion trillion). This number is called Avogadro's number.

Why Do We Need the Mole?

Think of it like a "dozen" but WAY bigger. A dozen eggs = 12. A mole of eggs = 6.022 × 10²³. We use moles because atoms are so small that even a tiny amount of any substance contains an enormous number of them. One teaspoon of water contains roughly 1.67 × 10²³ molecules.

Definition: Molar Mass

The mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol). For an element, the molar mass equals the atomic mass number on the periodic table. For a compound, add up the atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula.

Core Mole Conversions
moles = grams ÷ molar mass grams = moles × molar mass particles = moles × 6.022 × 10²³
Example: Molar mass of water (H₂O) = 2(1) + 16 = 18 g/mol. So 36 grams of water = 36 ÷ 18 = 2 moles of water.

Stoichiometry — Using Equations to Predict Amounts

Definition: Stoichiometry

Using the balanced equation to figure out how much of one substance you need — or how much product you'll get. The coefficients in the balanced equation tell you the exact mole ratios between all substances.

Stoichiometry Road Map

Grams of A → moles of A → (use mole ratio from equation) → moles of B → grams of B

Example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. For every 1 mole of O₂ you use, you get 2 moles of H₂O.

Limiting Reagent — What Runs Out First?

In most reactions, one reactant runs out before the other. The one that runs out first is the limiting reagent — it controls (limits) how much product you can make. The leftover reactant is in excess. To find the limiting reagent: convert each reactant to moles of product — whichever gives the smaller amount of product is limiting.

Limiting Reagent Analogy

Making sandwiches: 1 sandwich needs 2 slices of bread + 1 slice of cheese. If you have 10 slices of bread but only 3 slices of cheese, you can only make 3 sandwiches. Cheese is the limiting reagent. Bread is in excess.

Percent Yield
% yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
Theoretical yield is what you calculated you should get. Actual yield is what you measured in the lab. Percent yield is always ≤ 100% because real experiments always have some loss.
✎ Worked Example — Mole Conversions — Grams ↔ Moles ↔ Particles
How many grams are in 0.50 moles of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)? Then: how many molecules is that? (Molar mass of glucose: C=12, H=1, O=16.)
1Step 1 — Find the molar mass of C₆H₁₂O₆: 6 carbons: 6 × 12 = 72.   12 hydrogens: 12 × 1 = 12.   6 oxygens: 6 × 16 = 96.   Total: 72 + 12 + 96 = 180 g/mol.
2Step 2 — Grams = moles × molar mass: 0.50 mol × 180 g/mol = 90 grams of glucose.
3Step 3 — Molecules = moles × Avogadro's number: 0.50 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³ molecules.
4Reading it out loud: 0.50 moles of glucose weighs 90 grams and contains about 300 billion trillion molecules. That's why we need the mole — atoms are impossibly small!
✓ Answer: Molar mass = 180 g/mol. 0.50 mol = 90 g = 3.0 × 10²³ molecules.
✎ Worked Example — Full Stoichiometry — Grams of A to Grams of B
How many grams of water (H₂O) form when 10.0 g of hydrogen gas (H₂) burns completely? Balanced equation: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. This is the most-tested calculation in introductory chemistry.
1Step 1 — Convert grams of H₂ to moles: Molar mass of H₂ = 2 × 1 = 2 g/mol.   Moles of H₂ = 10.0 g ÷ 2 g/mol = 5.0 mol H₂.
2Step 2 — Use the mole ratio from the equation: The balanced equation says 2 mol H₂ → 2 mol H₂O (ratio is 1:1). So 5.0 mol H₂ produces 5.0 mol H₂O.
3Step 3 — Convert moles of H₂O to grams: Molar mass of H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = 18 g/mol.   Grams = 5.0 mol × 18 g/mol = 90.0 grams of water.
4The road map (always in this order): Grams A → (÷ molar mass A) → Moles A → (× mole ratio) → Moles B → (× molar mass B) → Grams B.
5Limiting reagent check: If the problem gave you amounts of BOTH H₂ and O₂, you'd convert each to moles of H₂O and pick the SMALLER result. That reactant is the limiting reagent.
✓ Answer: 10.0 g H₂ → 5.0 mol H₂ → 5.0 mol H₂O → 90.0 g H₂O.

08 Gases — How Pressure, Volume, and Temperature Relate

Gases are unique: they fill whatever container they're in, and changing pressure, temperature, or amount directly affects volume. Several laws describe these relationships.

LawWhat it saysEquationReal-life example
Boyle's LawPressure ↑ means Volume ↓ (and vice versa), when temperature is constantP₁V₁ = P₂V₂Squeezing a syringe or a balloon — harder pressure = smaller volume
Charles's LawTemperature ↑ means Volume ↑, when pressure is constantV₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂Hot air balloon — heating air inside makes it expand and lift the balloon
Gay-Lussac's LawTemperature ↑ means Pressure ↑, when volume is constantP₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂Aerosol can warning: don't heat it — pressure builds and it can explode
Avogadro's LawMore gas molecules = bigger volume (at same temp and pressure)V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂Blowing up a balloon — more air in = bigger balloon
Ideal Gas Law — The Master Equation
PV = nRT
P = pressure (in atm) | V = volume (in liters) | n = moles of gas | R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K | T = temperature in KELVIN
ALWAYS Use Kelvin for Gas Law Problems!

Celsius temperatures can be negative (like −10°C), which would cause math errors in gas law equations. Kelvin is always positive. Convert: K = °C + 273. At STP (standard temperature and pressure = 0°C and 1 atm), 1 mole of any gas occupies 22.4 liters.

✎ Worked Example — Boyle's Law — A Syringe Problem
A syringe contains 60 mL of air at a pressure of 1.0 atm. You push the plunger until the pressure is 3.0 atm (temperature stays constant). What is the new volume?
1Identify the law: Pressure and volume change, temperature constant → Boyle's Law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
2List the knowns: P₁ = 1.0 atm, V₁ = 60 mL, P₂ = 3.0 atm, V₂ = ?
3Solve for V₂: V₂ = (P₁ × V₁) ÷ P₂ = (1.0 × 60) ÷ 3.0 = 20 mL.
4Sanity check: Pressure tripled, so volume should be 1/3 as big. 60 ÷ 3 = 20 mL ✓. When pressure goes UP, volume goes DOWN — inverse relationship.
✓ Answer: V₂ = 20 mL. (Pressure tripled → volume is 1/3 of original.)
✎ Worked Example — Ideal Gas Law — How Many Moles?
A sealed container holds a gas at 2.0 atm pressure, 5.0 L volume, and 300 K temperature. How many moles of gas are in the container? (R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K)
1Write the equation: PV = nRT. We want n (moles), so rearrange: n = PV ÷ (RT).
2Check units: P = 2.0 atm ✓, V = 5.0 L ✓, T = 300 K ✓ (already in Kelvin — good!), R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K.
3Plug in and calculate: n = (2.0 × 5.0) ÷ (0.0821 × 300) = 10.0 ÷ 24.63 ≈ 0.406 mol
4Always use Kelvin! If T had been given as 27°C, you MUST convert first: K = 27 + 273 = 300 K. Forgetting this is the #1 gas law mistake.
✓ Answer: n ≈ 0.41 moles of gas.

09 Solutions & Concentration — Mixing Things in Water

Key Terms

Solution: A homogeneous (uniform) mixture of two or more substances.

Solute: The substance being dissolved (usually present in smaller amount). Salt in saltwater = the solute.

Solvent: The substance doing the dissolving (usually present in larger amount). Water in saltwater = the solvent. Water is called the "universal solvent."

Molarity — The Most Common Concentration Unit
Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution
Example: If you dissolve 2 moles of NaCl in 1 liter of water, the solution is 2 M (2 molar). Molarity tells you "how concentrated is this solution?"
Dilution Equation — When You Add More Water
M₁V₁ = M₂V₂
The moles of solute stay the same when you dilute. You just add more solvent to spread them out. Used in labs when making solutions from concentrated stock solutions.

What Dissolves in What?

"Like Dissolves Like" — The Most Important Solubility Rule

Polar solvents (like water) dissolve polar solutes (like salt, sugar). Nonpolar solvents (like oil) dissolve nonpolar solutes (like grease). This is why "oil and water don't mix" — oil is nonpolar and water is polar. And why soap works: it has both a polar end and a nonpolar end, so it can grab onto grease AND water!

Electrolytes — Do Ions Form in Solution?

Definition: Electrolyte

A substance that conducts electricity when dissolved in water because it produces ions in solution.

TypeWhat happens in waterConducts electricity?Examples
Strong electrolyteBreaks apart completely into ions (100% dissociation)WellNaCl, HCl, NaOH, KBr
Weak electrolyteOnly partially breaks apart into ionsPoorlyAcetic acid (vinegar), ammonia
NonelectrolyteStays as whole molecules — no ionsNot at allSugar, ethanol (alcohol)
✎ Worked Example — Calculating Molarity — Making a Solution
A student dissolves 29.25 g of NaCl into enough water to make 500 mL (0.500 L) of solution. What is the molarity? (Molar mass of NaCl = 58.5 g/mol)
1Step 1 — Convert grams to moles: moles = grams ÷ molar mass = 29.25 ÷ 58.5 = 0.50 mol NaCl.
2Step 2 — Apply the molarity formula: M = mol solute ÷ L solution = 0.50 mol ÷ 0.500 L = 1.0 M.
3What does 1.0 M mean? Every liter of this solution contains 1.0 mole of NaCl. Roughly the same saltiness as seawater (which is about 0.6 M NaCl).
✓ Answer: 1.0 M NaCl solution.
✎ Worked Example — Dilution — Making a Weaker Solution from a Strong One
A lab has a 6.0 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) stock solution. How much of the stock do you need to make 250 mL of a 0.50 M solution?
1The concept: Dilution means adding solvent (water). The moles of solute don't change — you're just spreading them through more liquid. Equation: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂.
2List knowns: M₁ = 6.0 M (stock), V₁ = ? (what we want), M₂ = 0.50 M (diluted), V₂ = 250 mL.
3Solve for V₁: V₁ = (M₂ × V₂) ÷ M₁ = (0.50 × 250) ÷ 6.0 = 125 ÷ 6.0 ≈ 20.8 mL of stock acid.
4Lab procedure: Measure 20.8 mL of the stock solution, pour it into a flask, then add water until the total volume reaches 250 mL. ALWAYS add acid TO water, never water to acid.
5Sanity check: The diluted solution (0.50 M) is 12× weaker than the stock (6.0 M). Volume increased 250/20.8 ≈ 12× bigger. ✓
✓ Answer: V₁ = 20.8 mL of the 6.0 M stock solution.

10 Acids, Bases & pH

Definition: Acid (Arrhenius)

A substance that produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water. The more H⁺ ions, the more acidic.

Definition: Base (Arrhenius)

A substance that produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) when dissolved in water. The more OH⁻ ions, the more basic (also called alkaline).

The Brønsted-Lowry definition is broader: an acid is a proton donor (gives away H⁺), and a base is a proton acceptor (accepts H⁺).

PropertyAcidsBases
Taste (DON'T test this!)Sour (lemon juice, vinegar)Bitter (baking soda)
Feel (DON'T test this!)StingingSlippery/soapy
Litmus paper testTurns blue litmus redTurns red litmus blue
pH valueLess than 7Greater than 7

The pH Scale

Definition: pH

A scale from 0 to 14 that measures how acidic or basic a solution is. pH 7 = neutral (pure water). Below 7 = acidic. Above 7 = basic. The formula: pH = −log[H⁺].

The pH Scale Is Logarithmic — Each Step Is 10× Bigger

A solution at pH 3 is NOT twice as acidic as pH 6. It is 10³ = 1,000 times more acidic. Each pH unit represents a 10-fold change in hydrogen ion concentration. Battery acid (pH ~0) is about 10 billion times more acidic than pure water (pH 7).

Everyday pH Examples

pH 0–2: Battery acid, stomach acid | pH 3–4: Lemon juice, vinegar | pH 5–6: Coffee, rain | pH 7: Pure water | pH 8–9: Baking soda, seawater | pH 11–12: Ammonia | pH 14: Drain cleaner

Strong vs. Weak Acids and Bases

Strong vs. Weak

Strong acid/base: Dissociates completely in water — every molecule breaks apart into ions. These are more dangerous and more reactive.

Weak acid/base: Only partially dissociates — most molecules stay intact. Vinegar (acetic acid) is a weak acid — it still burns but not nearly as badly as a strong acid.

The 6 Strong Acids — Memorize These

HCl (hydrochloric), HBr (hydrobromic), HI (hydroiodic), HNO₃ (nitric), H₂SO₄ (sulfuric), HClO₄ (perchloric). Everything else is a weak acid. Strong bases: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂.

Neutralization Reaction
acid + base → salt + water HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
This is how antacids work — the base in the tablet (like Mg(OH)₂) neutralizes stomach acid (HCl), reducing heartburn.
Definition: Buffer

A solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Your blood is a buffer — it stays between pH 7.35 and 7.45 no matter what you eat. A buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base + conjugate acid).

✎ Worked Example — pH Calculations — Finding pH from [H⁺] and Vice Versa
Three questions that cover everything the exam asks about pH math: (A) [H⁺] = 0.001 M — find pH. (B) pH = 9 — is this acidic or basic, and what is [H⁺]? (C) Orange juice has [H⁺] = 3.16 × 10⁻⁴ M — find pH.
1A — [H⁺] = 0.001 M → pH: pH = −log[H⁺] = −log(0.001) = −log(10⁻³) = −(−3) = pH 3. Acidic (below 7). This is about the acidity of vinegar.
2B — pH 9 → [H⁺] and type: pH 9 is above 7 → basic. [H⁺] = 10⁻pH = 10⁻⁹ = 0.000000001 M. Very few hydrogen ions — baking soda is around pH 9.
3C — [H⁺] = 3.16 × 10⁻⁴ M → pH: pH = −log(3.16 × 10⁻⁴). Use log rule: log(3.16 × 10⁻⁴) = log(3.16) + log(10⁻⁴) ≈ 0.5 + (−4) = −3.5. So pH = −(−3.5) = pH 3.5. Between 3 and 4 — makes sense for orange juice!
4The pattern: Every power of 10 in [H⁺] is one pH unit. [H⁺] = 10⁻⁵ → pH 5. [H⁺] = 10⁻¹² → pH 12. If the concentration is a 'clean' power of 10, pH = just the exponent (as a positive number).
✓ Answer: A: pH 3. B: basic, [H⁺] = 10⁻⁹ M. C: pH 3.5.
✎ Worked Example — Neutralization — Will the Result Be Acidic, Basic, or Neutral?
50 mL of 0.10 M HCl is mixed with 50 mL of 0.10 M NaOH. What happens? Then: what if only 25 mL of NaOH is used instead?
1Part 1 — Equal volumes and concentrations: Moles of HCl = 0.050 L × 0.10 mol/L = 0.005 mol. Moles of NaOH = 0.050 L × 0.10 mol/L = 0.005 mol. Reaction: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. Equal moles react completely — all acid and base consumed. Result: neutral solution, pH = 7 (just saltwater).
2Part 2 — Only 25 mL NaOH: Moles HCl = 0.005 mol. Moles NaOH = 0.025 L × 0.10 = 0.0025 mol. NaOH runs out first (limiting). It neutralizes 0.0025 mol HCl, leaving 0.005 − 0.0025 = 0.0025 mol HCl unreacted. Total volume = 50 + 25 = 75 mL = 0.075 L. [H⁺] = 0.0025 ÷ 0.075 ≈ 0.033 M. pH = −log(0.033) ≈ pH 1.5 (still quite acidic).
3Key principle: Neutralization is just like stoichiometry — compare moles of acid and moles of base. Whichever is less is the limiting reagent. Excess of either determines the final pH.
✓ Answer: Equal moles → pH 7 (neutral). Half as much base → pH 1.5 (acidic — excess HCl remains).

11 Nuclear Chemistry — Changes in the Nucleus

Nuclear Chemistry vs. Regular Chemistry

Regular chemical reactions involve only the electrons of atoms. Nuclear reactions involve the nucleus itself — changing the number of protons or neutrons. This can transform one element into a completely different element!

Definition: Radioactive Decay

When an unstable nucleus spontaneously releases energy and particles to become more stable. The atom often transforms into a different element in the process.

Three Types of Radiation

TypeWhat it isChargePenetrating powerWhat stops itEffect on nucleus
Alpha (α)Helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons)+2LowestA sheet of paper; your skinMass # −4, atomic # −2
Beta (β)A high-speed electron shot from the nucleus−1MediumAluminum foilAtomic # +1 (a neutron became a proton)
Gamma (γ)High-energy wave (like very powerful X-ray)0HighestThick lead or concreteNo change in mass or atomic number
Real-World Radiation

Alpha: Smoke detectors use alpha radiation (safe indoors because paper/air stops it). Radon gas in basements emits alpha — dangerous because you breathe it into your lungs where nothing can stop it.

Beta: Used in some medical treatments to target tumors.

Gamma: Sterilizing medical equipment, cancer radiation therapy, airport security scanners.

Half-Life — How Fast Does Radioactivity Decrease?

Definition: Half-Life

The time it takes for half of a radioactive sample to decay. After one half-life, 50% remains. After two half-lives, 25% remains. After three, 12.5%. And so on.

Half-Life Formula
Amount remaining = initial amount × (1/2)ⁿ
n = number of half-lives elapsed = (total time) ÷ (length of one half-life)
Half-Life Example

You start with 80 grams of a radioactive substance. Its half-life is 10 years. After 30 years (3 half-lives): 80 × (1/2)³ = 80 × 1/8 = 10 grams remaining.

Carbon-14 has a half-life of ~5,730 years — that's why it can be used to date objects thousands of years old!

Fission vs. Fusion

Nuclear FissionNuclear Fusion
What happensA large, heavy nucleus splits into smaller onesSmall, light nuclei combine into a larger one
ExampleUranium-235 splits in nuclear reactors and atomic bombsHydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium in the Sun
Energy released?Yes — huge amountsYes — even more than fission
Where usedNuclear power plants, nuclear weaponsThe Sun, hydrogen bombs; scientists working to harness it for clean energy
Where Does All That Energy Come From? E = mc²

In both fission and fusion, the products weigh slightly less than the starting materials. That "missing" mass isn't actually missing — it was converted directly into energy, following Einstein's famous equation E = mc². Because the speed of light (c) is so enormous, even a tiny bit of mass converts to a massive amount of energy.

✎ Worked Example — Half-Life — Three Types of Problems
A hospital uses iodine-131 (half-life = 8 days) for thyroid treatment. A patient receives a 40 mCi dose. (A) How much remains after 24 days? (B) How long until only 5 mCi remains? (C) What fraction remains after 5 half-lives?
1A — Amount after 24 days: Number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 half-lives. Amount = 40 × (1/2)³ = 40 × 1/8 = 5 mCi. Quick check: 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 ✓
2B — Time to reach 5 mCi: How many times must you halve 40 to get 5? 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 = 3 halvings = 3 half-lives. Time = 3 × 8 days = 24 days. (Same answer as part A — makes sense!)
3C — Fraction after 5 half-lives: Amount = initial × (1/2)⁵ = (1/2)⁵ = 1/32. So 1/32 (about 3.1%) remains.
4Pattern to memorize: 1 half-life → 1/2 left. 2 → 1/4. 3 → 1/8. 4 → 1/16. 5 → 1/32. Each step: multiply by 1/2 again. Never reaches zero — it just gets smaller and smaller.
✓ Answer: A: 5 mCi after 24 days. B: 24 days. C: 1/32 remains.
